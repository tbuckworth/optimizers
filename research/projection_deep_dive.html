Gradient Projection — Deep Dive

A 10-parameter worked example with intuition about what the eigenvectors actually mean

1. First: Why Exactly p Eigenvectors?

This is not a coincidence — it's a basic fact from linear algebra. The gradient covariance matrix is p × p (one row and column per parameter). Any real symmetric p × p matrix has exactly p eigenvectors forming an orthonormal basis for ℝp. Every vector in the space can be uniquely decomposed as a weighted sum of these p eigenvectors.

With 4 parameters, the covariance is 4×4 and you get 4 eigenvectors. With 235,146 parameters (our MNIST network), you get 235,146 eigenvectors. We keep the top k (e.g. 200) and discard the rest. The "discarding" is the filtering.

2. What Does an Eigenvector of the Gradient Covariance Mean?

The covariance matrix C tracks: across many training steps, when weight i's gradient is large, is weight j's gradient also consistently large (or consistently negative)?

Entry C[i,j] is the covariance of the gradient of weight i with the gradient of weight j, measured over time (across batches). Positive means they tend to both go up or both go down together. Negative means when one goes up, the other goes down. Near-zero means they're independent.

An eigenvector of this matrix identifies a group of weights whose gradients move together as a coherent unit. The eigenvector's entries tell you the relative participation of each weight in that pattern:

  • A large positive entry means that weight participates strongly, moving in the same direction as others with positive entries
  • A large negative entry means that weight participates strongly but in the opposite direction — it goes down when the positives go up. This is still part of the pattern. Anti-correlation is signal, not noise.
  • A near-zero entry means that weight doesn't participate in this particular co-movement pattern

The eigenvalue tells you how much total gradient variance is explained by that pattern — how strongly and consistently it recurs across training steps.

The key point: this is about correlations between weights, measured over time. A top eigenvector says: "across many different batches, these weights' gradients consistently co-vary in this pattern." It's not about any single batch — it's about what patterns repeat.

94.2% of the total gradient variance is explained by just 2 directions out of 10. The remaining 8 directions share the last 5.8%. With rank k = 2, we keep only e1 and e2.

What e1 is saying: "Across many training steps, weights 1–4 tend to get positive gradients at the same time as weights 5–7 get negative gradients. This is a pattern that keeps recurring." The anti-correlation (w1–4 vs w5–7) is part of the signal — these weights are locked in an opposing relationship that recurs consistently. Weights 8–10 don't participate in this pattern (near-zero entries).

What e2 is saying: "Independently of the first pattern, weights 8–10 tend to all get positive gradients together, sometimes with w3–4 joining in."

What e3–e10 are saying: "There are also these other co-movement patterns, but they're weak and inconsistent — they explain very little variance." Our hypothesis is that these correspond to sample-specific memorisation or noise.

4.1 What VVT Actually Is

The projection matrix P = VVT is what gets multiplied with the gradient. Let me spell out exactly what VVT means, because the notation can be confusing.

V is 10×2 (ten rows, two columns). VT is 2×10. Their product VVT is 10×10.

It equals e1e1T + e2e2T. Each eieiT is an outer product: a column vector times a row vector, giving a matrix. Let me show this for the first 3 weights:

e1 for w1,w2,w3 = [+0.461, +0.410, +0.358]

e1 · e1T = [ +0.461 ] × [ +0.461   +0.410   +0.358 ]
        [ +0.410 ]
        [ +0.358 ]

      = [ 0.461×0.461   0.461×0.410   0.461×0.358 ]
       [ 0.410×0.461   0.410×0.410   0.410×0.358 ]
       [ 0.358×0.461   0.358×0.410   0.358×0.358 ]

      = [ +0.212   +0.189   +0.165 ]
       [ +0.189   +0.168   +0.147 ]
       [ +0.165   +0.147   +0.128 ]

Then you do the same for e2 and add them together. The result is the projection matrix P. When you multiply P · g, each entry of the output is a weighted combination of all 10 gradient values — but the weights are determined by the eigenvectors, so only the "patterned" parts of the gradient survive.

5. What Happens to Each Weight

This is the key question. Let's take a realistic mixed gradient and trace what happens to every single weight.

Suppose at some training step, the gradient is:

g = [+1.52, +0.67, +0.71, +1.16, -0.43, -0.76, +0.49, +1.01, +0.17, +0.29]

This gradient decomposes as: 2.0 · e1 + 1.0 · e2 (signal) + 1.0 · e3 + 0.5 · e5 + 0.7 · e7 (noise).

5.1 Step-by-step projection

Step 1: Compute VTg — how much of g lies along each kept eigenvector:

e1 · g = 0.461(1.52) + 0.410(0.67) + 0.358(0.71) + 0.389(1.16) − 0.328(0.43) − 0.369(0.76) − 0.307(0.49) + ... = 2.0
e2 · g = −0.048(1.52) − 0.171(0.67) + 0.231(0.71) + 0.203(1.16) + 0.122(0.43) + ... + 0.486(0.29) = 1.0

Step 2: Reconstruct: gfiltered = 2.0 · e1 + 1.0 · e2

6. Three Things the Filter Can Do to a Weight

The examples above show that the filter doesn't simply "keep or zero" individual weights. It can do three different things:

  1. Preserve (w2, w6): The weight's gradient was already aligned with the recurring patterns. The filter barely changes it.
  2. Amplify (w3, w9, w10): The weight participates in a strong recurring pattern, but this particular batch under-represented it. The filter "fills in" what the pattern predicts, increasing the update.
  3. Override (w7): The weight's raw gradient pointed in the wrong direction relative to the recurring patterns. The filter flips the direction entirely. This is the strongest intervention — it's saying "I don't care what this batch said about w7, the persistent pattern says w7 should go down."
So no, the memorising weights don't just get zero. Individual weights don't get "zeroed" — the filter operates on the gradient as a whole vector. It projects the entire gradient onto the signal subspace. Each weight ends up with whatever update the signal patterns prescribe for it, regardless of what the raw gradient said. A weight could get its update increased, decreased, flipped, or (if it doesn't participate in any signal pattern) effectively zeroed.

7. A Mostly-Noise Gradient

For comparison, here's a gradient that lies mostly in the noise directions (e3–e10):

g_noise = [-0.51, +0.47, +0.79, -1.14, -0.48, -0.36, +0.15, +0.93, -0.81, +0.05] g_filtered = [+0.05, +0.05, +0.02, +0.03, -0.04, -0.04, -0.04, -0.02, -0.03, -0.02]

Only 0.3% of the gradient energy survives. The 10 weights each get an update around ±0.04 instead of their original ±0.5 to ±1.1. The gradient is effectively destroyed — not because each weight was zeroed individually, but because the overall direction didn't match any recurring pattern.

8. What "Recurring Across Batches" Actually Means

To be precise about what the covariance captures: it's about which weights tend to have correlated gradients, measured over many training steps.

It is not about each weight being consistent with itself across batches (that would be the mean, which we subtract out). It's about the relationships between weights: when w1's gradient is unusually large this step, is w2's also unusually large? Is w5's unusually negative? These cross-weight patterns, measured across time, are what the covariance captures.

An eigenvector with large eigenvalue says: "There is a particular pattern of relative weight movements that keeps recurring across many different batches." The entries of the eigenvector give the relative participation — positive entries move together, negative entries move opposite. Both positive and negative correlations are part of the signal. An eigenvector like e1 with entries [+.46, +.41, ..., −.33, −.37, ...] captures a consistent anti-correlation between the two groups.

9. Honest Uncertainty

The mechanism described above — "top eigenvectors = signal, bottom = memorisation noise" — is our hypothesis for why the filter works. The experimental results are consistent with it: the filter demonstrably resists memorising corrupted labels on MNIST (+4% test accuracy over Adam at 40% label noise). But we should be clear about what we don't know:

  • We haven't proven the eigenvector decomposition separates signal from noise. It's plausible and the experiments support it, but we don't have a theorem. Other explanations could exist.
  • We've only tested on MNIST with a 235K-parameter MLP. We don't know if the effect holds for larger models, harder datasets, or different architectures.
  • The 0.5% accuracy cost on clean data might grow. On harder tasks, the filtering might be too aggressive and hurt performance more.
  • "Noise directions are transient" is an assumption. If corrupted-label gradients happen to align with the signal eigenspace (perhaps because they share low-level features), the filter wouldn't catch them.
  • The examples above use a made-up covariance. We chose it to be illustrative. The real covariance matrix of a 235K-parameter network might have different structure — we haven't inspected it in detail.
