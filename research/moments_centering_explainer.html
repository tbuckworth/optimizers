Moments & centering

What the gradient covariance actually captures, the centered-vs-uncentered choice, and how each might map to signal vs noise — with an honest account of what we've actually run.

Companion to the spectral-filter write-up

1. First moment vs second moment

Take the gradient vector g ∈ ℝ^p (flattened over all parameters), treated as a random quantity — random because it varies across the examples in a batch, and across training steps.

  • First moment = the mean, μ = E[g]. A single vector: "the typical / average push." It's a direction (and magnitude) in parameter space.
  • Second moment = the matrix M = E[g gᵀ] (p×p). Its diagonal entry M_ii = E[g_i²] is the average squared gradient of parameter i (how big it is); its off-diagonal M_ij = E[g_i g_j] is how parameters i and j's gradients move together. So the second moment is the natural object for "what co-varies with what."

"Moment" is just the statistician's word for an average of a power: the first moment averages g (power 1), the second averages products g·gᵀ (power 2). The first is a vector; the second is a matrix because there's a product for every pair of parameters.

2. Centered vs uncentered — one identity says it all

The covariance is the second moment with the mean removed:

C = E[(g − μ)(g − μ)ᵀ] = E[g gᵀ] − μ μᵀ   ⟹   M = C + μ μᵀ

That identity is the whole story. The uncentered second moment M equals the centered covariance C plus the outer product of the mean. So:

  • Uncentered (M = E[ggᵀ]) keeps the mean's contribution. Its top eigenvector is the direction maximizing E[(vᵀg)²] — the direction of largest average energy. If the mean push μ is large, the top eigenvector is pulled toward μ: roughly "the dominant direction the gradient points, including the shared push."
  • Centered (C) subtracts μμᵀ first. Its top eigenvector maximizes Var(vᵀg) — the direction of largest fluctuation around the mean: "the direction the gradient most deviates along."

One-line intuition. Suppose every example pushes roughly the same way (a big shared μ) plus small idiosyncratic wiggles. Then the uncentered top eigenvector ≈ the shared push, while the centered top eigenvector ≈ the biggest wiggle. They can point in completely different — even orthogonal — directions.

3. The part we kept fuzzy: there are two axes, and we only ever moved both at once

"Centered vs uncentered" is one axis. But there's a second, independent axis hiding in our two implementations: what population are we averaging over?

  • Across time steps (temporal). Accumulate the batch-mean gradient over successive steps into an EMA. The covariance then measures how the full-batch gradient direction fluctuates from step to step. — this is what WeightCovarianceFilterV2 does.
  • Across examples (within a step). Take the per-sample gradients gᵢ inside one batch and average gᵢgᵢᵀ. The covariance then measures how examples agree/disagree right now. — this is what PerSampleCovarianceFilter does.

Important confound. Our two optimizers differ on both axes at once:
• V2 = temporal + centered (covary batch-mean gradients over steps, subtract the running mean).
• per-sample = cross-example + uncentered (covary per-sample gradients within a step, no mean subtraction).
So we have never isolated the effect of centering on its own — any difference between the two is also a difference between "over time" and "over examples." Keep that in mind for §5.

4. How each might correspond to signal vs noise (speculative)

This is the interesting and genuinely unsettled part. Different framings disagree about which end is the signal.

(a) Uncentered, across examples — the "coherent gradients" reading

The top eigenvector of the uncentered per-sample second moment ≈ the direction the examples most agree on (the mean, since μμᵀ dominates when examples are coherent). Chatterjee's coherent gradients hypothesis says exactly this is the generalizing part: patterns common to many examples reinforce in the sum and get learned first; idiosyncratic, per-example directions cancel out and sit in the low-energy tail = memorization. Reading: signal = the agreed (top) direction; noise = the disagreeing tail. → keep the top.

(b) Centered, across examples — the "disagreement = memorization" reading (NOT run)

If instead you center the per-sample gradients (subtract the batch mean) and keep high-variance directions, you are keeping the directions of maximum disagreement between examples. Those are plausibly the memorization directions (where each example wants something different). Under this reading you'd want to remove the centered top directions, not keep them. Centering flips the intuition versus (a). We have not run this.

(c) Centered, across time — what we actually mostly ran (V2)

V2 removes the running-average push and keeps the directions in which the full-batch gradient is currently changing / co-fluctuating the most over recent steps. The interpretation is murkier than (a): high temporal co-fluctuation could be the learning frontier (the directions actively being figured out — good to keep) or could be instability. Empirically V2 behaves like a coherence amplifier — it helps when the useful thing is the coherent thing (noise robustness, grokking on modular addition) and hurts when the useful signal is weak and not yet dominant (sparse parity). But note this is the centered-temporal object, which is not the clean "agreement" object of (a).

The honest summary of the theory. "Project onto the top covariance directions" only obviously corresponds to "keep signal, drop noise" under reading (a) — uncentered, across examples (coherent gradients). The centered objects keep fluctuation/disagreement directions, whose status as signal or noise depends on whether you're covarying over examples (likely noise → should remove) or over time (ambiguous). We have been somewhat loose about which object we mean, and they are not interchangeable.

Reading this table honestly:

  • ~99% of the runs are the centered (temporal, batch-mean) V2. The uncentered object appears in a single experiment family — the per-sample parity filter — which never groked. (It also shows up as the direction-estimator in the supervised backdoor-ablation work, but that's a different mechanism, not the filter.)
  • The one uncentered datum we have: on parity its top eigenvector was ≈ the mean gradient (measured cos ≈ 0.999 for a shared direction) and it never groked — consistent with "the top agreed direction is the bulk-fitting push, not the 3-bit signal." But that's parity, where nothing in the gradient top helps.
  • We have no clean A/B of centering. Because V2 (centered+temporal) and the per-sample filter (uncentered+cross-example) differ on both axes, we cannot attribute any result to centering alone. The centered-vs-uncentered question is, in honesty, essentially untested in isolation.

If we wanted to actually test it (cheap, one knob): run the same population — say per-sample, across examples — once uncentered (keep the mean = reading (a)) and once centered (keep disagreement = reading (b)), on the same task, and compare. That isolates the mean-outer-product term μμᵀ and would tell us directly whether "keep the agreed direction" or "keep the fluctuation" is the one that tracks generalization. Likewise we could add a centering toggle to V2 to isolate it in the temporal setting.
