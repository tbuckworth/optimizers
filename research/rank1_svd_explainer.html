Estimating the top-\(k\) eigenvectors of the gradient covariance — without ever building it

1. Why we can't just build the covariance

We train a network with \(p\) parameters. Flatten every gradient into a vector \(g\in\mathbb{R}^p\). We are interested in the covariance of that gradient over training,

$$ C \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[(g-\bar g)(g-\bar g)^\top\big] \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{p\times p}. $$

Its top eigenvectors are the directions in weight-space along which the gradient consistently points — the "consensus" directions. That is the object the whole filter is built around.

The trouble is size. \(C\) is \(p\times p\). For the small CNN in this project \(p = 591{,}274\), so

$$ p^2 \;=\; 591{,}274^2 \;\approx\; 3.5\times 10^{11}\ \text{entries} \;\approx\; 1.4\ \text{TB in float32}. $$

We cannot store it, let alone run a \(p^3\) eigendecomposition on it. And we don't need to: we only ever use the top \(k\) eigenvectors (here \(k\) up to a few hundred). So the goal is to track a running, low-rank estimate of \(C\)'s leading eigenspace, updating it one cheap step at a time. That is exactly what a streaming (incremental) rank-1 SVD does.

2. What we actually want to maintain

We keep a thin factorisation of the current covariance estimate:

$$ C \;\approx\; V\,\operatorname{diag}(s^2)\,V^\top, \qquad V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}\ \text{(orthonormal columns)},\quad s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\ge 0}. $$
  • \(V\) — the current top-\(k\) eigenvectors (each a unit vector in weight-space). This is the only big object, and it is \(p\times k\), not \(p\times p\).
  • \(s\) — the corresponding singular values; \(s_i^2\) is the eigenvalue (the variance captured along \(V_{:,i}\)).

Every training step gives us one new (centred) gradient vector \(w\in\mathbb{R}^p\). We want to fold it into the estimate with an exponential moving average (decay \(\lambda\), close to 1),

$$ C' \;=\; \lambda\,C \;+\; (1-\lambda)\,w\,w^\top, $$

and read off the new top-\(k\) eigenvectors \(V'\), \(s'\). The point of everything below is that this update touches only \(O(pk)\) numbers, never \(p^2\).

Intuition for the EMA. \(\lambda\) sets the memory. A direction has to keep showing up over roughly \(\tfrac{1}{1-\lambda}\) steps to stay in the top eigenspace; transient one-off directions decay away. With \(\lambda=0.99\) that window is \(\sim 100\) steps — which, as we'll see in §10, also caps how many directions the estimate can really hold.

3. The key trick: an EMA covariance is a thin stacked matrix

Here is the move that makes it all cheap. A matrix of the form "\(M^\top M\)" is automatically symmetric positive-semidefinite, and its eigenvectors are the right singular vectors of \(M\). So let's write our current estimate as exactly such a product. Define the \(k\times p\) "factor"

$$ M \;=\; \operatorname{diag}(s)\,V^\top \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M^\top M \;=\; V\operatorname{diag}(s^2)V^\top \;=\; C. $$

Row \(i\) of \(M\) is just \(s_i\,V_{:,i}^\top\). Now do the EMA update. Because \(\lambda C = (\sqrt\lambda M)^\top(\sqrt\lambda M)\) and \((1-\lambda)ww^\top = \big(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\,w^\top\big)^\top\big(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\,w^\top\big)\), we can write the updated covariance as one stacked product:

$$ C' \;=\; \lambda C + (1-\lambda)ww^\top \;=\; M'^\top M', \qquad M' \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{\lambda}\,M \\[2pt] \sqrt{1-\lambda}\,w^\top \end{bmatrix}\ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times p}. $$

So \(M'\) is the old factor scaled by \(\sqrt\lambda\), with one extra row — the new gradient scaled by \(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\) — stapled to the bottom. \(C'\) has rank at most \(k+1\). We've turned "update a \(p\times p\) matrix" into "append a row to a \((k{+}1)\times p\) factor." We still won't form even that \((k{+}1)\times p\) factor explicitly — but conceptually it's the whole story.

4. The small \((k{+}1)\times(k{+}1)\) Gram matrix

The nonzero eigenvalues of \(C' = M'^\top M'\) (a huge \(p\times p\) matrix) are identical to the eigenvalues of the tiny Gram matrix

$$ G \;=\; M' M'^\top \ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}. $$

(This is the standard SVD fact: \(M'^\top M'\) and \(M'M'^\top\) share nonzero eigenvalues; one is \(p\times p\), the other \((k{+}1)\times(k{+}1)\).) And \(G\) has a clean closed form. Let

$$ c \;=\; V^\top w \ \in\ \mathbb{R}^k \quad(\text{the new gradient's coordinates in the current basis}). $$

Write the rows of \(M'\) explicitly: row \(i

BlockEntryValue
top-left \(k\times k\)\(G_{ij}=\sqrt\lambda s_i V_{:,i}^\top \cdot \sqrt\lambda s_j V_{:,j}\)\(\lambda\, s_i s_j\,\delta_{ij} = \lambda\,\operatorname{diag}(s^2)\)
last column / row\(G_{ik}=\sqrt\lambda s_i V_{:,i}^\top\cdot\sqrt{1-\lambda}\,w\)\(\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\; s_i\, c_i\)
bottom-right \(1\times 1\)\(G_{kk}=(1-\lambda)\,w^\top w\)\((1-\lambda)\,\lVert w\rVert^2\)

So, with \(\sqrt\lambda\) and \(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\) abbreviated sd, sn:

$$ G \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \lambda\,\operatorname{diag}(s^2) & \sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\,(s\odot c)\\[4pt] \sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\,(s\odot c)^\top & (1-\lambda)\,\lVert w\rVert^2 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Every quantity here is cheap: \(c=V^\top w\) costs \(O(pk)\), \(\lVert w\rVert^2\) costs \(O(p)\), and the rest is \(O(k)\). We never touched a \(p\times p\) object. Now eigendecompose the little matrix:

$$ G \;=\; U\,\Lambda\,U^\top, \qquad U\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)},\ \ \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_{k+1}). $$

The new singular values are \(s' = \sqrt{\Lambda}\) (keep the top \(k\), drop any that are numerically zero). What remains is to turn \(U\) — eigenvectors of the small matrix — back into eigenvectors of the big one.

5. Recovering the new eigenvectors without materializing anything big

If \(M' = W\Sigma Z^\top\) is the SVD of the factor, then \(G=M'M'^\top = W\Sigma^2W^\top\) (so \(U=W\), \(\Lambda=\Sigma^2\)) and the eigenvectors we want are the right singular vectors \(Z\). Rearranging \(M'=W\Sigma Z^\top\) gives

$$ V' \;=\; Z \;=\; M'^\top\,U\,\Lambda^{-1/2}. $$

Now expand \(M'^\top\). Its columns are the rows of \(M'\), i.e. \(M'^\top = \big[\;\sqrt\lambda\,V\operatorname{diag}(s)\ \big|\ \sqrt{1-\lambda}\,w\;\big]\) — a \(p\times(k+1)\) matrix that is just our existing \(V\) and the single new vector \(w\). Let \(\text{coeffs} = U\,\Lambda^{-1/2}\) (shape \((k{+}1)\times k\)). Splitting it into its first \(k\) rows and its last row:

$$ \boxed{\;V' \;=\; \underbrace{V\,\big(\sqrt\lambda\, s \odot \text{coeffs}_{[:k]}\big)}_{\text{rotate the old basis}} \;+\; \underbrace{w\,\big(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\,\text{coeffs}_{[k]}\big)}_{\text{inject the new direction}}\;} $$

That's the whole update. The first term is a \((p\times k)\cdot(k\times k)\) matmul — it rotates and reweights the existing eigenvectors. The second is an outer product of the new gradient \(w\) with a length-\(k\) row — it folds in whatever part of \(w\) was new. Notice we never orthogonalised \(w\) by hand and never built the residual explicitly: the small eigendecomposition does the orthogonalisation for us, and \(w\) appears directly in the reconstruction. (The component of \(w\) already inside \(\operatorname{span}(V)\) is handled automatically through \(c\); the component orthogonal to it enters through the same \(w\) term.)

The payoff in one line. One new gradient → one tiny \((k{+}1)\times(k{+}1)\) eigendecomposition → a rotation of the old eigenvectors plus a rank-1 injection. No \(p\times p\) matrix, no \(p^3\) eig, ever.

6. The full algorithm + the real code

Pseudocode for one step (given the new centred gradient \(w\)):

1.  c       = Vᵀ w                      # (k,)      coordinates of w in current basis     O(pk)
2.  build G = [[ λ·diag(s²),       √(λ(1−λ))·(s⊙c) ],
               [ √(λ(1−λ))·(s⊙c)ᵀ, (1−λ)·‖w‖²       ]]     # (k+1, k+1)                   O(k)
3.  Λ, U    = eigh(G);  sort desc;  drop Λ ≤ ε;  keep top k            # small eig         O(k³)
4.  s'      = sqrt(Λ)
5.  coeffs  = U / s'                    # (k+1, k')  =  U · Λ^(−1/2)
6.  V'      = V · (√λ · s ⊙ coeffs[:k]) + w · (√(1−λ) · coeffs[k])     # rotate + inject   O(pk²)
7.  V, s    = V', s'

And the actual implementation (experiments/weight_cov_optimizer_v2.py, _update_svd), lightly trimmed. The variable g_centered is our \(w\); sd=\(\sqrt\lambda\), sn=\(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\):

def _update_svd(self, g):
    # --- EMA running mean, then centre: w = g - mean ---
    if self.grad_mean is None:
        self.grad_mean = g.detach().clone()
    else:
        self.grad_mean.mul_(self.decay).add_(g.detach(), alpha=1 - self.decay)
    g_centered = g.detach() - self.grad_mean                      # w  (p,)

    if self.V is None:                                            # bootstrap: first vector
        norm = g_centered.norm()
        if norm > 1e-12:
            self.V = (g_centered / norm).unsqueeze(1)             # (p, 1)
            self.S = norm.unsqueeze(0).cpu()
        return

    k  = self.V.shape[1]
    sd = math.sqrt(self.decay)                                    # √λ
    sn = math.sqrt(1 - self.decay)                                # √(1−λ)

    c          = self.V.T @ g_centered                            # (k,)   = Vᵀw          O(pk)
    g_norm_sq  = g_centered.dot(g_centered).item()                # ‖w‖²
    S_cpu, c_cpu = self.S, c.cpu()

    # --- assemble the (k+1)×(k+1) Gram matrix ---
    gram = torch.zeros(k + 1, k + 1)
    gram[:k, :k] = torch.diag(sd * sd * S_cpu * S_cpu)            # λ·diag(s²)
    cross        = sd * sn * S_cpu * c_cpu                        # √(λ(1−λ))·(s⊙c)
    gram[:k, k]  = cross
    gram[k, :k]  = cross
    gram[k, k]   = sn * sn * g_norm_sq                            # (1−λ)·‖w‖²

    # --- tiny eigendecomposition, sort desc, prune ---
    eigvals, eigvecs = torch.linalg.eigh(gram)
    eigvals = eigvals.flip(0);  eigvecs = eigvecs.flip(1)
    pos = eigvals > 1e-12
    eigvals, eigvecs = eigvals[pos], eigvecs[:, pos]
    new_k   = min(self.rank, len(eigvals))
    eigvals = eigvals[:new_k];  eigvecs = eigvecs[:, :new_k]      # (k+1, new_k)
    s_new   = eigvals.sqrt()

    # --- reconstruct V' = rotate(old V) + inject(w),  no (k+1)×p matrix built ---
    coeffs     = eigvecs / s_new.unsqueeze(0)                     # U·Λ^(−1/2)   (k+1, new_k)
    top_coeffs = (sd * S_cpu.unsqueeze(1) * coeffs[:k]).to(g.device)   # (k, new_k)
    bot_coeffs = (sn * coeffs[k]).to(g.device)                        # (new_k,)
    V_new = self.V @ top_coeffs + g_centered.unsqueeze(1) * bot_coeffs.unsqueeze(0)

    self.V = V_new.contiguous()
    self.S = s_new

The only object that ever has a \(p\) in its shape is \(V\) (and the single vector \(w\)). The eigendecomposition is on a matrix whose size is set by \(k\), not \(p\).

7. A small worked example you can check by hand

Take \(p=3\), decay \(\lambda=0.5\) (so \(\sqrt\lambda=\sqrt{0.5}\)), and suppose centring is already done (mean \(=0\)) so we can focus on the SVD mechanics. Start with a rank-1 estimate pointing along \(e_1\) with singular value \(s=2\):

$$ V=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad s=[\,2\,],\qquad\text{so } C = V\,s^2\,V^\top = 4\,e_1e_1^\top. $$

A new centred gradient arrives, orthogonal to the current basis: \(w=[0,3,0]^\top\). Then the true updated covariance is

$$ C' = \lambda C + (1-\lambda)ww^\top = 0.5\cdot 4\,e_1e_1^\top + 0.5\cdot 9\,e_2e_2^\top = 2\,e_1e_1^\top + 4.5\,e_2e_2^\top. $$

So by eye the answer must be: eigenvalues \(\{4.5, 2\}\), eigenvectors \(\{e_2, e_1\}\) — the new direction \(e_2\) is now dominant. Let's confirm the algorithm reproduces it.

Step 1. \(c = V^\top w = [\,0\,]\) (orthogonal), \(\lVert w\rVert^2 = 9\).

Step 2. Gram matrix (\(k=1\), so \(2\times 2\)):

$$ G=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda s^2 & \sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\,s\,c\\ \cdot & (1-\lambda)\lVert w\rVert^2\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}0.5\cdot 4 & 0\\ 0 & 0.5\cdot 9\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\ 0 & 4.5\end{bmatrix}. $$

Step 3. Eigenvalues of \(G\), sorted descending: \(\Lambda=\{4.5,\,2\}\), with eigenvectors \(U=\big[\,[0,1]^\top,\ [1,0]^\top\,\big]\) (the column \([0,1]^\top\) goes with \(4.5\)). So \(s' = \sqrt{\{4.5,2\}} = \{2.121,\,1.414\}\). ✓ eigenvalues already match.

Step 5–6. \(\text{coeffs}=U/s'\): column 0 \(=[0,1]^\top/2.121=[0,\,0.4714]^\top\); column 1 \(=[1,0]^\top/1.414=[0.7071,\,0]^\top\). Reconstruct, with \(\sqrt\lambda=0.7071\), old \(s=2\):

  • Column 0 (new dominant): old-basis term \(=0.7071\cdot V\cdot 2\cdot \text{coeffs}_{[:1],0}=0.7071\cdot 2\cdot 0\cdot e_1 = 0\); inject term \(=0.7071\cdot w\cdot \text{coeffs}_{[1],0}=0.7071\cdot[0,3,0]^\top\cdot 0.4714=[0,1,0]^\top\). Sum \(=e_2\). ✓
  • Column 1: old-basis term \(=0.7071\cdot 2\cdot 0.7071\cdot e_1 = e_1\); inject term \(=0.7071\cdot[0,3,0]^\top\cdot 0 = 0\). Sum \(=e_1\). ✓

So \(V'=[\,e_2\ |\ e_1\,]\), \(s'=\{2.121,1.414\}\) — exactly the covariance we wrote down by inspection, and the basis grew from rank 1 to rank 2 and reordered itself, all from the \(2\times 2\) Gram. If \(w\) had not been orthogonal (\(c\neq 0\)), the only change is that \(G\) gets nonzero off-diagonals and \(V'\) comes out as a genuine rotation mixing \(e_1\) and the new direction — same machinery.

8. A note on centering (the running mean)

We track the covariance of \(g\) about its mean, so we keep an EMA mean \(\mu \leftarrow \lambda\mu+(1-\lambda)g\) and feed the algorithm \(w = g-\mu\). One quirk worth knowing: on the very first step \(\mu\) is initialised to \(g\), so \(w=0\) and nothing is added — the first step just seeds the mean. From the second step on, \(w\) is the deviation of the current gradient from the recent average, which is what we want: a direction is "signal" only if it persists relative to the running average, not just because the average itself is large.

9. Complexity: time and memory

QuantityThis method (per step)Naive (build \(C\), eig)
Memory\(O(pk)\) — just \(V\)\(O(p^2)\) — the full \(C\)
Project \(c=V^\top w\)\(O(pk)\)—
Small eigendecomp of \(G\)\(O(k^3)\)\(O(p^3)\)
Reconstruct \(V'\)\(O(pk^2)\) (the \(V\!\cdot\!\text{coeffs}\) matmul dominates)—
Total / step\(\mathbf{O(pk^2 + k^3)}\)\(O(p^2)\) store + \(O(p^3)\) eig

Plug in \(p=591{,}274\) and a realistic working rank \(k\approx 100\): the reconstruction is \(\sim pk^2 \approx 6\times 10^{9}\) flops — a few milliseconds on a GPU — and the eig is on a \(101\times 101\) matrix. Measured cost on the project's CNN is \(\approx 7\) ms/step, roughly \(2\times\) plain Adam. The naive route would need 1.4 TB and a \(p^3\sim 2\times 10^{17}\)-flop eigendecomposition: not happening.

The decay caps the usable rank — raising \(k\) alone does nothing. Because the estimate only remembers \(\sim\tfrac{1}{1-\lambda}\) steps, it can populate at most about that many independent directions. With \(\lambda=0.99\) (window \(\approx100\)) the basis saturates near \(\sim100\) columns no matter how large you set \(k\): the extra eigenvalues fall below the \(10^{-12}\) prune threshold and are dropped. Empirically on the CNN, \(k=200,\,1000,\,4000\) all converge to \(\sim100\) live columns. To genuinely use a higher rank you must also lengthen the memory (raise \(\lambda\)): \(\lambda=0.999\) opens up \(\sim1000\) dimensions, etc. So "more parameters need more rank" must be implemented as "raise \(\lambda\) and \(k\) together," not \(k\) alone.

10. Extension: updating with per-sample gradients (a rank-\(B\) update)

Everything above used one vector per step: the batch-mean gradient \(w\). That makes the covariance a temporal one — it measures how the mean gradient varies from step to step. An alternative (and the thing worth trying for finding a backdoor / reward-hack direction) is to feed the \(B\) per-sample gradients of the batch, \(w_1,\dots,w_B\), and track how the gradient varies across examples. This is entirely possible — it just turns the rank-1 update into a rank-\(B\) one.

The math change

The EMA update now folds in \(B\) outer products at once:

$$ C' \;=\; \lambda\,C \;+\; \frac{1-\lambda}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} w_b w_b^\top. $$

Stack the per-sample (centred) gradients as columns of \(W_s=[\,w_1\,\cdots\,w_B\,]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times B}\). The factor gains \(B\) extra rows instead of one:

$$ M' = \begin{bmatrix}\sqrt\lambda\,\operatorname{diag}(s)V^\top\\[2pt] \sqrt{\tfrac{1-\lambda}{B}}\;W_s^\top\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+B)\times p},\qquad G=M'M'^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+B)\times(k+B)}. $$

The Gram matrix keeps the same three-block shape, with the scalar bottom-right corner now a \(B\times B\) block. Writing \(P = V^\top W_s\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times B}\) (the projections of all \(B\) samples) :

$$ G=\begin{bmatrix} \lambda\,\operatorname{diag}(s^2) & \sqrt{\tfrac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{B}}\,\operatorname{diag}(s)\,P\\[6pt] \sqrt{\tfrac{\lambda(1-\lambda)}{B}}\,P^\top\!\operatorname{diag}(s) & \tfrac{1-\lambda}{B}\,W_s^\top W_s \end{bmatrix}. $$

The bottom-right \(W_s^\top W_s\) is the \(B\times B\) Gram of the per-sample gradients — cheap because \(B\) (batch size, ~128) is small. Eigendecompose this \((k+B)\times(k+B)\) matrix, prune, and reconstruct exactly as before:

$$ V' = V\big(\sqrt\lambda\,s\odot \text{coeffs}_{[:k]}\big) + W_s\big(\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\lambda}{B}}\,\text{coeffs}_{[k:]}\big), $$

where now \(\text{coeffs}_{[k:]}\) is a \(B\times k'\) block and the second term is a \((p\times B)\cdot(B\times k')\) matmul. Same algorithm, "1" replaced by "\(B\)" throughout.

Why you'd want it

The batch-mean covariance can only see a direction if the average gradient moves along it. A signal shared by a subset of examples — e.g. the trigger→target direction carried by exactly the poisoned samples — partly averages out in the mean, but stands out strongly in the across-sample covariance, because those samples all push the same way while the rest cancel. Taking the top eigenvector(s) of the per-sample covariance is therefore a principled way to recover such a direction, and the eigenvalue spectrum tells you how many directions dominate (you expect a sharp drop after a few). This is exactly the "estimate \(d\) from the sample-level gradient covariance" idea, and it drops straight into the same rank-update machinery.

The real extra cost is usually step 1 — materialising \(B\) per-sample gradients is \(B\times\) the memory of one backward and needs torch.func.vmap(torch.func.grad(...)) (or functorch/BackPACK) rather than a plain .backward(). Everything downstream just grows the small matrix from \(k+1\) to \(k+B\), which is still tiny. So: yes, it's possible and it's the same derivation; the only price is computing per-sample gradients, and the reward is a covariance that resolves directions shared by subsets of examples rather than only those visible in the batch average.
