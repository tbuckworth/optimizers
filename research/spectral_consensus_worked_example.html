The Spectral Consensus Optimizer: A Worked Example

This document walks through the spectral consensus idea with concrete numbers. We'll compute everything by hand (well, with small matrices) to build intuition for why it works on real labels but fails on random labels.

1. The Setup

Scenario: We're training a tiny neural network with 3 parameters ($w_1, w_2, w_3$) to classify images. We have a mini-batch of 6 training samples. For each sample, we compute the gradient — a 3D vector telling us which direction to move each parameter to reduce that sample's loss.

In standard SGD, we'd average these 6 gradients and take a step. But what if some of these gradients are noise — pointing in directions that would help memorize individual samples but not learn generalizable features?

The spectral consensus idea: only update in directions where many samples agree.

2. The Gradients: Real Labels

Suppose 4 of our 6 samples are correctly labeled cats, and they share genuine visual features (whiskers, ears, fur texture). Their gradients should be correlated — they all "want" similar parameter changes. The other 2 samples have randomly assigned labels: a dog labeled "cat" and a cat labeled "dog." Their gradients will be unrelated to the real-label gradients.

4. Step 2: Build the Similarity Matrix

Step 2 Compute the $6 \times 6$ cosine similarity matrix $S$, where $S_{ij} = \hat{g}_i \cdot \hat{g}_j$ (the dot product of normalized gradients).

This number ranges from $-1$ (opposite directions) to $+1$ (same direction). $S_{ij} = 1$ on the diagonal (each vector is identical to itself).

$$S_{ij} = \hat{g}_i \cdot \hat{g}_j = \sum_{k=1}^{3} \hat{g}_{i,k} \cdot \hat{g}_{j,k}$$

For example, $S_{12} = (0.873)(0.802) + (0.436)(0.535) + (-0.218)(-0.267) = 0.700 + 0.233 + 0.058 = \mathbf{0.991}$.

5. Step 3: Eigendecompose the Similarity Matrix

Step 3 Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $S$. The eigenvalues tell us how much agreement exists. The eigenvectors tell us which groups of samples agree.

$$S = U \Lambda U^T$$

where $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_6)$ with $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_6$.

6. Step 4: Decide Which Eigenvalues Represent Real Agreement

Step 4a Not all eigenvalues represent genuine consensus. Even with completely random gradients, you'll get some eigenvalue spread just by chance. We need a threshold to distinguish "real agreement" from "random noise."

We use a simple rule called the Marchenko-Pastur criterion: compute the average eigenvalue, and keep only eigenvalues larger than $2\times$ the average.

The average eigenvalue is easy: since our similarity matrix $S$ has 1s on the diagonal (every vector has cosine similarity 1.0 with itself), the trace is $B = 6$, so:

$$\bar{\lambda} = \frac{\text{trace}(S)}{B} = \frac{6}{6} = 1.0$$ $$\text{threshold} = 2 \times \bar{\lambda} = 2.0$$

Why 2×? If all gradients were random unit vectors, the eigenvalues would cluster around 1.0 with some random spread. An eigenvalue needs to be well above this noise floor to represent genuine inter-sample agreement. The factor of 2 is a conservative choice — "at least twice the random expectation."

7. Step 5: Reweight Samples Based on Consensus

This is the core step, and it has three parts. Let's go through each one carefully.

7a. The Problem: Standard SGD Gives Equal Weight to Every Sample

In normal SGD, we average all 6 gradients with equal weight:

$$g_{\text{mean}} = \frac{1}{6}(g_1 + g_2 + g_3 + g_4 + g_5 + g_6)$$

This is equivalent to using a uniform weight vector:

$$w_{\text{uniform}} = \left[\frac{1}{6},\ \frac{1}{6},\ \frac{1}{6},\ \frac{1}{6},\ \frac{1}{6},\ \frac{1}{6}\right]$$

The problem: samples 5 and 6 (random labels) get just as much say as samples 1–4 (real labels). Their noisy gradients pollute the update.

7b. The Idea: Adjust Weights Using the Eigenvectors

We want to adjust those weights so that samples in the consensus group (1–4) get more weight, and outsider samples (5–6) get less. The eigenvectors from step 3 tell us exactly how to do this.

Recall the top eigenvector $u_1$ (the one with the dominant eigenvalue):

ComponentValueMeaning

This vector acts like a "membership score" — it tells us how much each sample participates in the dominant agreement pattern. Samples 1–4 have large, same-sign values (they're all in the consensus). Samples 5–6 have different values (they're not part of it).

7c. The Mechanics: Projection Onto the Consensus Direction

We want to take our uniform weights $w_{\text{uniform}} = [1/6, 1/6, \ldots, 1/6]$ and adjust them to only reflect the consensus direction. Mathematically, this is a projection.

"Projection" just means: take the part of the uniform weights that lines up with the top eigenvector(s), and throw away the rest.

With $k$ kept eigenvectors (let's say $k=1$ for now, just the top one), the projection works in two sub-steps:

Sub-step 1: How much does the uniform vector align with $u_1$? Compute their dot product:

$$\alpha_1 = u_1 \cdot w_{\text{uniform}} = \sum_{i=1}^{6} u_{1,i} \times \frac{1}{6}$$

Sub-step 2: Scale the eigenvector by this alignment to get the new weights:

$$w_{\text{consensus}} = \alpha_1 \times u_1$$

This gives us a weight for each sample:

What just happened: We started with equal weights for all 6 samples. By projecting onto the consensus eigenvector, we've automatically upweighted samples that agree with each other (1–4) and downweighted samples that don't (5–6). Nobody told the algorithm which labels were real — it figured this out purely from gradient agreement.

If $k > 1$ (multiple eigenvalues above threshold), we'd repeat this for each kept eigenvector and add up the contributions: $w_{\text{consensus}} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} (u_j \cdot w_{\text{uniform}}) \times u_j$. This is what the compact formula $U_k U_k^T \frac{\mathbf{1}}{B}$ means — it does all $k$ projections at once using matrix multiplication.

7d. Compute the Consensus Gradient

Now we simply take the weighted sum of the original (un-normalized) gradients using our new consensus weights:

$$g_{\text{consensus}} = \sum_{i=1}^{6} w_{\text{consensus},i} \times g_i$$

      10. Why This Matters

      This is the core mechanism:

      1. Real features create gradient consensus. When multiple samples share genuine structure (edges, textures, semantic features), their gradients point in similar directions. This shows up as a dominant eigenvalue in the similarity matrix.
      2. Random labels destroy consensus. With random labels, there's no shared structure to learn. Gradients point in arbitrary directions, producing a flat eigenvalue spectrum. No direction has more agreement than random chance.
      3. Spectral filtering exploits this difference. By keeping only eigencomponents above the noise floor, the optimizer automatically retains the real-feature signal and discards the memorization signal. The result: an optimizer with a built-in inability to memorize random labels.

      This connects to a deep question in deep learning theory. Zhang et al. (2016) showed that standard networks can memorize completely random labels — and nobody could explain why they don't memorize in practice (when labels are real). The spectral consensus optimizer offers a constructive answer: if you filter by consensus, memorization becomes impossible because there's nothing to agree on.

      The punchline: An optimizer that only learns what the data agrees on cannot learn from noise — because noise, by definition, is what the data doesn't agree on.

      11. From Toy Example to Practical Implementation

      The worked example above shows the clean, idealized version of the idea. The actual code implementation makes several choices and modifications worth understanding.

      11a. This Is a Gradient Filter, Not an Optimizer

      The spectral consensus mechanism is not a standalone optimizer. It's a gradient filter that wraps any existing optimizer. The workflow is:

      1. Compute per-sample gradients (the expensive part)
      2. Run the spectral consensus filter to produce a single "consensus gradient"
      3. Inject that gradient into the model's .grad fields
      4. Call the base optimizer's .step() — e.g. SGD with momentum

      So yes, momentum is used — by the base optimizer in step 4. The spectral filter only decides what gradient to feed to that optimizer. In our experiments, the base optimizer is SGD with momentum 0.9 and learning rate 0.05. The spectral filter replaces only the gradient computation; everything else (momentum tracking, learning rate, weight updates) is handled by SGD as normal.

      In principle, you could wrap Adam, Lion, or any other optimizer as the base — the filter is agnostic.

      11b. Per-Sample Gradients via vmap

      The biggest practical challenge is computing a separate gradient for each sample in the batch. Standard PyTorch's loss.backward() only gives you the average gradient over the batch. We need individual gradients.

      We use PyTorch's torch.func.vmap and grad_and_value to efficiently compute per-sample gradients in parallel on the GPU. For a batch of $B$ samples and $p$ parameters, this gives us the full $B \times p$ gradient matrix $G$ — the raw material for everything above. This is the main computational overhead: roughly $B\times$ the cost of a single backward pass (though vmap makes it more efficient than literally doing $B$ separate passes).

      11c. Normalization: Cosine Similarity

      In step 1 of the worked example, we normalized each gradient to unit length before building the similarity matrix. This means we're using cosine similarity (direction only) rather than raw dot products (direction × magnitude).

      Why? Because we care about whether samples agree on which direction to move the parameters, not about how much each sample's loss changed. A sample with a large gradient (high loss) shouldn't dominate the consensus just because it has large magnitude — it should be listened to if and only if other samples agree with its direction.

      The un-normalized gradients are used later when computing the final consensus gradient (step 7d) — we only normalize for the purpose of measuring agreement.

      11d. Hard vs Soft Thresholding

      The worked example uses a hard cutoff: eigenvalues above $2\times$ the mean are kept, everything below is completely discarded. In practice, this can be too aggressive — it throws away signal from eigenvalues that are above the noise floor but below the threshold.

      In our experiments, the hard cutoff ($k=1$ or $k=2$ on MNIST) dropped real-label accuracy from 99.7% to 93.8% — the filter was too strict and threw away some genuine signal.

      The soft thresholding variant addresses this by using a smooth sigmoid function instead of a hard cutoff:

      $$\alpha_i = \sigma\!\left(\frac{\lambda_i - \text{threshold}}{\text{temperature}}\right)$$

      where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function and temperature controls the sharpness of the transition. Each eigenvector's contribution is then scaled by $\alpha_i$ instead of being binary keep/discard. Eigenvalues well above the threshold get $\alpha \approx 1$, those well below get $\alpha \approx 0$, and those near the boundary get partial weight.

      11e. What We Learned from Experiments

      We ran all experiments on MNIST (digit classification) and a 2D spiral dataset, comparing SGD, Adam, AdamW, Lion, Muon, and the spectral consensus optimizer:

      • Real labels: All standard optimizers reach ~99%+ on MNIST. Spectral with hard threshold reaches 93.8% — good but degraded by aggressive filtering.
      • Random labels (the key test): After 50 epochs, Adam/AdamW memorize to ~40-50% train accuracy. SGD reaches ~30%. Spectral stays near chance (~14%) — it genuinely cannot memorize.
      • Surprise finding: The eigenvalue spectrum isn't as clean as this toy example suggests. Even with random labels, there's some eigenvalue structure because the input images (MNIST digits) share visual features regardless of labels. The gradients depend on both inputs AND labels, so random labels don't produce a perfectly flat spectrum. The consensus filter still works (can't memorize), but the mechanism is subtler than "flat = noise, peaked = signal."

      11f. Open Questions

      • Cost: Per-sample gradients are expensive. For large models and large batches, the $B \times p$ gradient matrix becomes enormous. Is there a way to approximate the spectral filter without computing full per-sample gradients?
      • Soft threshold tuning: The sigmoid temperature is a hyperparameter. Can it be set adaptively?
      • Interaction with real memorization: Real-world training involves some memorization (specific training examples are partially memorized). The spectral filter suppresses this — is that always desirable?
      • Label-conditioned filtering: Instead of one big similarity matrix, could we build separate matrices per predicted class? This might capture more nuanced consensus structure.
