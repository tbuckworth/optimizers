The Spectral Consensus Optimizer: A Worked Example

This document walks through the spectral consensus idea with concrete numbers. We'll compute everything by hand (well, with small matrices) to build intuition for why it works on real labels but fails on random labels.

1. The Setup

Scenario: We're training a tiny neural network with 3 parameters ($w_1, w_2, w_3$) to classify images. We have a mini-batch of 6 training samples. For each sample, we compute the gradient — a 3D vector telling us which direction to move each parameter to reduce that sample's loss.

In standard SGD, we'd average these 6 gradients and take a step. But what if some of these gradients are noise — pointing in directions that would help memorize individual samples but not learn generalizable features?

The spectral consensus idea: only update in directions where many samples agree.

2. The Gradients: Real Labels

Suppose 4 of our 6 samples are correctly labeled cats, and they share genuine visual features (whiskers, ears, fur texture). Their gradients should be correlated — they all "want" similar parameter changes. The other 2 samples have randomly assigned labels: a dog labeled "cat" and a cat labeled "dog." Their gradients will be unrelated to the real-label gradients.

4. Step 2: Build the Similarity Matrix

Step 2 Compute the $6 \times 6$ cosine similarity matrix $S$, where $S_{ij} = \hat{g}_i \cdot \hat{g}_j$ (the dot product of normalized gradients).

This number ranges from $-1$ (opposite directions) to $+1$ (same direction). $S_{ij} = 1$ on the diagonal (each vector is identical to itself).

$$S_{ij} = \hat{g}_i \cdot \hat{g}_j = \sum_{k=1}^{3} \hat{g}_{i,k} \cdot \hat{g}_{j,k}$$

For example, $S_{12} = (0.873)(0.802) + (0.436)(0.535) + (-0.218)(-0.267) = 0.700 + 0.233 + 0.058 = \mathbf{0.991}$.

Key pattern: The top-left $4 \times 4$ block (samples 1–4, all real labels) is filled with values near $1.0$ — these samples strongly agree. The bottom-right entries and the cross-terms with samples 5–6 are much smaller or negative. The real-label samples form a consensus cluster; the random-label samples are outsiders.

5. Step 3: Eigendecompose the Similarity Matrix

Step 3 Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $S$. The eigenvalues tell us how much agreement exists. The eigenvectors tell us which groups of samples agree.

$$S = U \Lambda U^T$$

where $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_6)$ with $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_6$.

6. Step 4: Filter the Gradient

Step 4 Keep only eigencomponents with eigenvalues significantly above the noise floor. We use the Marchenko-Pastur criterion: keep eigenvalues $> 2 \times \bar{\lambda}$, where $\bar{\lambda} = \text{trace}(S) / B = 6/6 = 1.0$.

So we keep eigenvalues $> 2.0$.

      9. Why This Matters

      This is the core mechanism:

      1. Real features create gradient consensus. When multiple samples share genuine structure (edges, textures, semantic features), their gradients point in similar directions. This shows up as a dominant eigenvalue in the similarity matrix.
      2. Random labels destroy consensus. With random labels, there's no shared structure to learn. Gradients point in arbitrary directions, producing a flat eigenvalue spectrum. No direction has more agreement than random chance.
      3. Spectral filtering exploits this difference. By keeping only eigencomponents above the noise floor, the optimizer automatically retains the real-feature signal and discards the memorization signal. The result: an optimizer with a built-in inability to memorize random labels.

      This connects to a deep question in deep learning theory. Zhang et al. (2016) showed that standard networks can memorize completely random labels — and nobody could explain why they don't memorize in practice (when labels are real). The spectral consensus optimizer offers a constructive answer: if you filter by consensus, memorization becomes impossible because there's nothing to agree on.

      The punchline: An optimizer that only learns what the data agrees on cannot learn from noise — because noise, by definition, is what the data doesn't agree on.
